# Costs: fees and tax

Balanced portfolio, 2003-09-29 to 2026-07-21 (22.8 years). Gross, a £100,000 investment grew to £495,698 (a 7.3% CAGR).

## Fees - a certain, compounding drag

| Annual fee | Final value of £100,000 | Lost to fees | Share of the gain lost |
|-----------|----------------------------|--------------|------------------------|
| 0.5% | £442,231 | £53,467 | 14% |
| 1.0% | £394,304 | £101,394 | 26% |
| 1.5% | £351,367 | £144,331 | 36% |

A 1% annual fee turned a £495,698 result into £394,304, a loss of £101,394 on a £100,000 investment over 23 years. The fee is only 1% a year, but it compounds against the whole pot every year, so the damage is far larger than 1%. This is the one cost a client can see and control, and it is often the largest certain number in the whole plan.

## Tax - the wrapper decision (illustrative)

Illustrative only, for a UK higher-rate taxpayer: assume the portfolio yields 2.5% a year in income taxed at 35% in a taxable account, plus 24% capital gains tax on the final gain. An ISA pays neither. Fees are set aside here so the tax effect is isolated.

The robust part is the recurring income tax. Taxing 2.5% of income at 35% acts like an extra ~0.87% annual fee. Over 23 years that alone cost about £89,901, on top of any management fee, and an ISA avoids it entirely. That is a similar size to a 1% management fee.

Capital gains tax is on top but is more controllable. Realising the whole gain in one year would add roughly £73,391 of CGT, but phasing disposals across tax years, using the annual exemption, and using both spouses' allowances all reduce it. An ISA removes it completely.

So the ISA wrapper was worth somewhere between about £89,901 (income tax only) and £163,292 (income tax plus a one-off CGT bill) on a £100,000 investment. The exact figure depends on allowances and how gains are realised, so read it as a scale. The point is that the wrapper decision is worth a large, fully controllable amount over a long horizon.

## Why this matters: fees raise goal risk

Fees do not only shrink a final pot. In drawdown they lower the probability the money lasts. A 1% fee is a certain reduction in return, so it is a certain increase in the chance of running out. Costs are the one lever here that is fully in the adviser's and client's control.
