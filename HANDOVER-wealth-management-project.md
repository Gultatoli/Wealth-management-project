# Handover Brief — Wealth Management Portfolio Project

**Purpose:** context for scoping and building a portfolio project. Read this first, then ask the open questions in the last section before writing any code.

**Date of handover:** 21 July 2026

---

## 1. Who this is for

- Final-year Biomedical Science student in the UK. Predicted 2:1. Graduating summer 2027.
- Final-year modules: pathophysiology of disease, exercise physiology, physiology and pharmacology. Lab practicals across all three involved designing data-collection protocols, plotting results, and writing up interpretations.
- Deliberately pivoting into **private-client wealth management** — specifically discretionary fund management (DFM), where the role combines running client portfolios with owning the client relationship. Not institutional asset management, not investment banking.
- Has an entrepreneurial background: has built and shipped apps, and has run a content business. Comfortable building things solo and seeing them through to launch.
- Technical level is unconfirmed — has shipped software but the stack, depth, and current fluency have not been established. **Ask before assuming.**

## 2. The goal this project serves

Applying to UK wealth management graduate schemes in **autumn 2026** for a **2027 intake**. Primary target is **Rathbones** (UK's largest discretionary wealth manager post-Investec merger). Secondary targets include RBC Brewin Dolphin, Brooks Macdonald, Quilter Cheviot and Evelyn Partners.

**Timeline is tight.** Applications open from early October 2026 — roughly ten weeks from this handover. Most are rolling, so applying in the first fortnight matters. Scope accordingly: something finished and modest beats something ambitious and half-built.

## 3. Why the project matters more than it looks

The CV has a section headed *Finance Experience & Independent Work*. It currently holds three items: a personal investment portfolio, a shadowing placement, and this project. The first two are aspirational and not yet done.

They were previously planning to study for the Investment Management Certificate (IMC), which would have carried most of the finance credibility on the page. **They are not currently studying for it and have asked for it to be left off entirely.** Do not reintroduce it.

The consequence: **this project is the main thing on the CV that evidences genuine, self-directed interest in investing.** It is load-bearing, not decorative. Treat it as the centrepiece of a job application, not a side experiment.

## 4. What the project has to achieve

In rough priority order:

1. **Be defensible in an interview.** They will be asked to explain it to a non-technical wealth manager in two minutes. Every decision inside it needs a reason they can articulate. Prefer a simple thing they fully understand to a sophisticated thing they can only partly explain.
2. **Produce a concrete output.** A conclusion, a finding, a working tool someone could actually use. "I explored some data" is not an output.
3. **Be specific to wealth management, not generic finance.** The differentiator is private-client thinking: risk profiling, suitability, long-horizon planning, drawdown, tax wrappers (ISA/pension), fees compounding over decades. A generic stock screener does not signal understanding of this industry; something engaging with how you serve a client over thirty years does.
4. **Be quantifiable on a CV line.** They need a bullet with a number in it. Design for that from the start.
5. **Play to the science background rather than hiding it.** Their genuine edge is methodology: hypothesis, controlled test, honest interpretation of results including negative ones. A project that shows rigour will read as more credible than one that shows enthusiasm.

## 5. Constraints

- Solo build, alongside final-year study starting September.
- No finance work experience yet, so the project cannot assume access to proprietary data or industry tooling. Public/free data sources only.
- Must survive scrutiny: if a number in it is wrong and an interviewer catches it, that is worse than not having the project.
- Should be shareable — a link, a repo, or a short write-up they can attach to an application.
- Avoid anything that could read as offering financial advice.

## 6. What good looks like

A finished, narrow project with an honest write-up. Something where the README or summary explains what question was asked, how it was tested, what came back, and what the limitations were. The limitations section matters — being able to say what the project *doesn't* prove is exactly the analytical maturity these firms are hiring for.

## 7. Open questions to ask before scoping

Do not skip these. Several materially change the design.

1. **Build or write?** A tool/app, or a piece of analysis with a written conclusion? They have not decided, and it pulls in different directions.
2. **Current technical stack and fluency** — what have they built before, in what, and how recently?
3. **Realistic weekly hours** between now and late September.
4. **Any specific investing interest already?** An asset class, a strategy, a question they are personally curious about. Genuine curiosity produces a better interview story than an assigned topic.
5. **Do they already have the personal portfolio running?** If so, the project could build on it rather than start fresh.
6. **Public or private?** Whether they want this visible on GitHub or as a private write-up affects how it is packaged.

## 8. Style notes for working with them

- They push back and check claims — one earlier cost figure was wrong and they caught it. Cite sources for anything factual; flag uncertainty rather than smoothing over it.
- They prefer direct assessment over hedged generalism.
- They have a humanizer skill they use to strip AI texture from written output. Any prose intended for the CV, a write-up, or an application should be plain and specific, and will likely be reviewed through it.
